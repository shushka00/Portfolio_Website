# How To Write Case Studies

Use this guide every time an agent writes, rewrites, reviews, or structures a portfolio case study in this repo.

Do not edit this file silently. Before changing `how-to-write-case-study.md`, explicitly ask the user for permission, unless the user has already asked in the current message to update this guide.

Reference template: Ghaida Zahran's Bundles case study, "Unlocking the revenue potential of Netflix Bundles." Use it as the writing-style, sequence, and format benchmark.

## Core Rule

A case study is not a diary of what was done. It is a proof of judgment.

Do not lead with operations, deliverables, or product-management mechanics. Lead with the user or business impact, then show how design created that impact.

Bad title:
"Merging Product A and Product B into one platform"

Why it fails:
This is operation-driven. It tells the reader what shipped, not why it mattered. A reader can reasonably ask: who cares that two products were merged?

Better title:
"Designing to reduce a 99% drop-off between signup and first successful outcome"

Why it works:
It names the outcome, stakes, and design problem. The merge becomes the means, not the headline.

## Title Formula

Use impact-led titles. The title should answer one of these:

- What user behavior changed?
- What business metric moved?
- What critical risk was reduced?
- What customer problem became solvable?
- What broken system did design make work?

Good patterns:

- "Designing to reduce drop-off from signup to first successful action"
- "Helping users complete a high-stakes workflow with more confidence"
- "Reducing time to first value from hours to minutes"
- "Unlocking revenue by helping customers make informed plan choices"

Avoid:

- "Merging X and Y"
- "Redesigning the dashboard"
- "Improving the user experience"
- "Building an ecosystem"
- "Creating a new information architecture"

Those can appear in the subtitle or body, but they should not carry the headline alone.

## Hard Style Rules From Portfolio Reviews

These are not preferences. Treat them as quality gates.

### 1. Never make the case study self-aware

Do not write about the case study as a case study inside the case study. The reader should stay inside the product problem, not be reminded that a portfolio artifact is being written.

Avoid:

- "This became the case-study headline."
- "This case study is about..."
- "The narrative shows..."
- "This section proves..."

Use instead:

- "Only 0.15% of signups reached a first published agent."
- "The metric showed the experience was failing before value was reached."

### 2. Do not foreground internal operations

Do not center phrases like merge, consolidation, alignment, handoff, platform unification, or ecosystem work unless they are clearly framed as the design solution. The ask should be stated as the user or business problem.

Avoid:

- "From merge two products to recover activation."
- "A product merge is an internal activity."
- "The work became a redesign claim."

Use instead:

- "Reduce drop-off before users reach the first working outcome."
- "Help users reach the build surface, complete setup, and publish with confidence."
- "Make the path from signup to value understandable."

### 3. No first-person process narration

Do not write "I did research," "I started by," "I moved," or similar process diary copy in page sections. The case study should read as outcome-led product writing, not a personal journal.

Use:

- "Research was conducted to identify why users dropped before publishing."
- "The audit focused on three moments: account creation, product routing, and publish confidence."
- "The research plan narrowed to the riskiest unanswered question."

Avoid:

- "I started with existing evidence."
- "I moved product audit ahead of interviews."
- "I treated the redesign as..."

First person is acceptable only in a dedicated Role section when clarifying ownership.

### 4. Explain domain ideas in plain language

If a non-domain reader would not understand the sentence, rewrite it. Avoid jargon without explanation.

Avoid:

- "The largest volume leak happened before the merge boundary, inside Console-owned account creation."
- "The seam was a UI framing problem."
- "The chassis won the shell."

Use instead:

- "Most people left before their account was ready."
- "Users had to leave the builder to find information the builder needed."
- "The same project appeared in two places, so users could not tell where to continue."

### 5. Avoid AI-slop contrast patterns

Do not use formulaic contrast language unless it is genuinely necessary.

Avoid:

- "not X, but Y"
- "not just X"
- "more than X"
- "from X to Y" when it sounds like a slogan
- em-dash-heavy sentences
- vague claims like "the work became four testable bets"

Use direct claims:

- "The goal was to reduce signup-to-publish drop-off."
- "Four hypotheses guided the design."
- "Research focused on the moments where users left."

## Opening Structure

Follow this order at the top of the case study:

1. Case study label
2. Impact-led title
3. One-sentence summary
4. Role, Company, Team, Timeline, and North star. Avoid generic Scope unless the user asks for it.

The summary must connect action to result:

Example:
"I introduced a plan selection screen into the bundle sign-up flow, improving upgrade rates and revenue for millions of global members."

For this portfolio, write like this:
"The redesigned flow helped users reach the first working outcome with less confusion and more confidence."

If metrics exist, include them early. If metrics are projected or the work is ongoing, say that honestly and name the committed measurement frame.

## Role And Team Section

Do not write vague role labels. Make ownership legible.

Use:

- Role: Principal Product Designer, solo designer, lead designer, or the exact role from the project
- Team: Product, engineering, data, support, sales, content, research, or other relevant partners
- Timeline: Exact month, quarter, year, or ongoing status
- North star: The user or business metric the design is trying to move
- Constraints: Frozen production screens, regulated environment, legacy systems, limited data, existing backend model, stakeholder pressure, or any real project constraint

Avoid generic "Scope" metadata at the top unless the reader truly needs it. Scope often reads like a task list. Put responsibilities in Role or body copy instead.

If the user was first, sole, lead, or embedded designer, say it. This matters because it explains the level of ambiguity and leadership required.

Good:
"Role: Principal Product Designer. Responsibility: strategy, IA, journey design, content, prototype, and handoff."

Weak:
"I worked on UX and UI."

## Standard Sequence

Use this sequence unless the user explicitly asks for a different one:

1. Overview
2. Context
3. The Gap
4. My Role
5. Discovery
6. Reframing
7. Hypotheses
8. Navigating Tension
9. Research
10. Solution
11. Results
12. Reflection

If the existing page has a different section order, preserve that page order but map the content to these functions. Do not rearrange live UI sections unless asked.

## Section Jobs

### Overview

Give the reader the whole case in 20 seconds:

- What problem existed
- Why it mattered
- What design changed
- What impact or measurement frame resulted

Do not start with background lore.

### Context

Explain the product, market, user, and business situation only as much as needed to understand the stakes.

Good context gives the reader enough to care. Bad context teaches the org chart.

### The Gap

Name the sharp problem.

This should be the moment where the reader understands the contradiction:

- Acquisition was strong, but upgrade behavior was weak.
- Signups were high, but almost nobody reached the first meaningful outcome.
- Two products shared one project record, but users experienced them as separate systems.

The Gap should be more specific than "the experience was confusing."

### My Role

State responsibility, level of ownership, collaborators, and constraints.

Use this section to show leadership:

- What did the designer own?
- What decisions did they influence?
- What ambiguity did they reduce?
- What constraints made the work harder?

### Discovery

Start with a question, not a task list.

Good:
"Discovery focused on what project meant in each product."

Weak:
"I conducted research, audited flows, and reviewed competitors."

Then show what evidence changed the understanding of the problem.

### Reframing

This is mandatory for strong strategic case studies.

Show the shift from business framing to human/product framing:

- Business frame: close the plan mix gap
- Better design frame: help members choose the best experience

- Business frame: combine two product surfaces
- Better design frame: reduce drop-off before users reach value

The reframe should make the solution feel inevitable.

### Hypotheses

Write hypotheses as bets, not tasks.

Include at least three hypotheses in a case study when the project involved meaningful product strategy. Three is the minimum because a strong case study should show that the designer considered multiple possible explanations, not just one favorite solution.

Do not confuse assumptions with hypotheses.

Assumption:
Something believed to be true, often based on context, stakeholder knowledge, prior research, or product constraints. It may shape the work, but it is not yet framed as a testable cause-and-effect claim.

Example:
"Users want to finish setup quickly."

Hypothesis:
A testable prediction that connects a design intervention to a user or business outcome.

Example:
"If we reduce setup decisions from four required choices to one required choice with reversible defaults, first-session completion will increase."

Each hypothesis should include:

- Stage of journey
- Assumption being tested
- Design intervention
- Success signal
- KPI or metric expected to move

Good:
"If users can make the riskiest decision in a lower-stakes environment first, completion quality and retention should increase."

Weak:
"Add a new screen."

Minimum hypothesis set:

1. Activation hypothesis: what will help users reach the first meaningful outcome?
2. Confidence or trust hypothesis: what will help users believe the product is safe, credible, or worth committing to?
3. Retention or revenue hypothesis: what will help the business capture durable value after the first action?

Optional additional hypotheses:

- Onboarding hypothesis
- Engagement hypothesis
- Expansion hypothesis
- Error recovery hypothesis
- Pricing or packaging hypothesis
- Accessibility hypothesis
- Support reduction hypothesis

Use this format:

| # | Stage | Assumption | Hypothesis | Design intervention | KPI |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| H1 | Activation | Users drop because the first decision is too ambiguous. | If we provide a clearer default path, more users will reach the first meaningful outcome. | Guided setup, templates, or progressive disclosure. | Completion rate, time to first value, drop-off rate. |
| H2 | Trust | Users hesitate when consequences are unclear. | If we explain consequences before commitment, users will complete higher-stakes actions with fewer reversals. | Preview, confirmation copy, rollback, cost visibility, or policy clarity. | Conversion quality, reversal rate, support tickets, retained completion. |
| H3 | Revenue or retention | Users do not pay or return unless value is visible soon after activation. | If the product shows value immediately after setup, retention or upgrade intent will improve. | Post-action summary, success state, next-best action, plan comparison, or usage insight. | Upgrade rate, retained users, repeat usage, expansion, churn reduction. |

Always replace the generic wording with the actual product, user, and metric.

### KPI And Measurement Assumptions

Every case study should have a KPI section or KPI paragraph when the work is tied to business or behavior change.

Use this sentence frame:

"By launching this design solution, we assume it will drive [metric] by [target amount] because [reason]."

Examples:

- "By launching this design solution, we assume it will increase first-session completion by 15% because users will make fewer unsupported setup decisions."
- "By launching this design solution, we assume it will reduce support tickets by 20% because the most common failure points are explained in-product."
- "By launching this design solution, we assume it will increase upgrade rate by 5% because users can compare value before committing."

If the metric is not yet validated, label it as a target, assumption, or measurement plan. Do not present it as a result.

KPI section should include:

- Primary metric: the main behavior or business outcome the design should move
- Secondary metrics: supporting signals that explain whether the design is working
- Guardrail metrics: what must not get worse
- Target: expected direction and amount of movement
- Time window: when the metric should be measured

Good:
"Primary KPI: first successful workflow completion. Target: +15% within 30 days of launch. Guardrail: no more than a 2% increase in support contact rate."

Weak:
"Success will be measured by engagement."

### Navigating Tension

Include real disagreement, tradeoffs, or constraints.

This is where the case study proves seniority. Strong design work has pressure:

- Revenue vs trust
- Speed vs confidence
- Frozen codebase vs needed change
- Short-term conversion vs long-term retention
- Stakeholder request vs user harm

Do not sanitize the tension into "we collaborated closely." Name the decision and why it held.

### Research

Do not dump methods. Explain why each method was used and what changed because of it.

Use a table only when it helps scan:

- Method
- Participants or source
- Why
- What it revealed
- Limitation

Prefer cheap existing signals before interviews when the product already exists. Interviews should answer what logs, support tickets, product data, heuristic review, or docs cannot.

### Solution

Present the solution as a response to evidence.

Use this pattern:

- Insight
- Design response
- Why it mattered

Avoid showing screens without explaining the decision behind them. A visual is not the argument; it supports the argument.

### Results

Lead with measurable impact when available.

Good:

- +5% upgrade rate
- -0.7% activation rate, named as an acceptable tradeoff
- 99.85% drop-off identified and tied to measurement plan
- Retained completion selected over raw completion when quality matters

If the work has not launched, do not fake impact. Use:

- Prototype shipped
- Build/typecheck passed
- Decision log completed
- Measurement frame committed
- Open engineering questions identified

Be honest about tradeoffs. A small negative metric can strengthen the story if the tradeoff is understood.

### Reflection

Do not end with generic gratitude or "I learned collaboration is important."

Reflection should include:

- What you would do differently
- What you would keep
- What principle the project reinforced
- What this reveals about your design judgment

Good:
"The way you frame the problem determines the quality of the solution."

## Writing Style

Write with clarity, restraint, and consequence.

Use:

- Short paragraphs
- Specific numbers
- Plain language
- Strategic verbs: reframed, narrowed, tested, validated, protected, reduced
- Strong section headlines that make an argument

Avoid:

- Corporate filler
- "Seamless"
- "Leveraged"
- "Worked closely"
- "Improved the UX"
- Process theater
- Long lists of deliverables with no consequence

The tone should feel senior, direct, and reflective. It should not sound like a pitch deck or a design school project.

## Image And Caption Rules

Every image needs a job.

Captions should explain why the image matters, not merely what it shows.

Weak:
"Plan selection screen."

Better:
"The included plan stayed visible at $0 extra, but no higher plan was pre-selected. This preserved opt-in choice while making upgrade value comparable."

Use images to show:

- The broken moment
- The decision point
- The tested concepts
- The final flow
- The measurable outcome

## Metrics Rules

Use metrics responsibly.

Do:

- Put the most important metric near the top.
- Explain the metric in human terms.
- Name tradeoffs.
- Distinguish shipped results from target metrics.
- Prefer behavior metrics over vanity metrics.

Do not:

- Claim launch impact for ongoing work.
- Use time-on-page as success unless the goal is attention.
- Hide negative movement if it is part of an intentional tradeoff.
- Count raw completion when retention or quality matters more.

Metric principle:
Choose the metric that represents durable user or business value, not the easiest number to inflate.

For example, "completed and retained" is stronger than "completed" when immediate reversal, churn, cancellation, refund, abandonment, or support escalation would make raw completion misleading.

## Checklist Before Publishing

Ask these questions before finalizing any case study:

- Does the title communicate impact, not just activity?
- Can a recruiter understand the case in 20 seconds?
- Is the business problem reframed as a user/product problem?
- Is the designer's ownership unmistakable?
- Does each section advance the argument?
- Are visuals explained by captions with a point of view?
- Are metrics honest and specific?
- Is there at least one meaningful tension or tradeoff?
- Does the solution follow from discovery, not appear randomly?
- Does the reflection reveal judgment?
